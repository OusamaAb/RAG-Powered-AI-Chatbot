# CPS714 — Software Project Management

## Course Overview
CPS714: Software Project Management focused on the planning, organization, execution, monitoring, and closing of software engineering projects. The course introduced the main challenges involved in managing large and complex software projects, including scope control, schedule planning, cost estimation, stakeholder communication, risk management, Agile practices, and project team coordination.

The course connected software engineering work with industry-standard project management techniques. Instead of only focusing on writing code, the course emphasized how software teams plan work, estimate effort, manage uncertainty, communicate progress, handle changing requirements, and deliver a project successfully within real-world constraints.

## Theory Learned
This course taught the theory behind managing software projects from the beginning of the project life cycle to final delivery and closure. A major concept learned was that a project is a temporary effort created to produce a unique product, service, or result. Unlike regular operations, projects have a defined goal, a beginning, an end, stakeholders, constraints, uncertainty, and measurable success criteria.

A key idea in the course was the triple constraint of project management: scope, time, and cost. The course explained how changes in one area usually affect the others. For example, increasing project scope may require more time, more people, or a higher budget. The course also expanded this idea by considering quality, risk, resources, and stakeholder satisfaction as important parts of project success.

The course introduced the difference between project management, program management, and portfolio management. Project management focuses on delivering a specific project. Program management coordinates related projects to gain broader benefits. Portfolio management focuses on selecting and managing projects that align with an organization’s strategic goals.

Another major theory area was project selection and strategic alignment. The course covered how organizations cannot pursue every possible project because of limited time, budget, and resources. Because of this, projects must be evaluated using business strategy, financial analysis, SWOT analysis, weighted scoring models, balanced scorecards, project priority, and expected value.

The course also explained different development approaches and project life cycles. Predictive or waterfall approaches are more structured and plan-heavy, while Agile and adaptive approaches allow more flexibility and feedback during development. Iterative development focuses on repeated refinement, while incremental development builds the product in smaller working pieces. Hybrid approaches combine predictive planning with Agile execution when a project needs both structure and flexibility.

A large part of the theory focused on project planning. This included creating a project management plan, defining the scope, identifying requirements, building a Work Breakdown Structure, creating schedule baselines, estimating costs, and planning communication. The project management plan was treated as the central document that coordinates all other planning areas and guides execution, monitoring, and control.

The course also covered scheduling theory. This included defining activities, identifying dependencies, creating activity lists, using milestones, estimating duration, building network diagrams, and using tools like Gantt charts. Concepts such as lead, lag, effort, duration, PERT, and three-point estimation were used to understand how project managers predict timelines under uncertainty.

Agile theory was another important part of the course. The course discussed Agile values, Scrum, sprint planning, sprint reviews, retrospectives, product backlogs, sprint backlogs, burndown charts, and team communication. Agile was presented as a way to manage changing requirements, improve feedback, and deliver value in smaller increments.

The course also introduced risk management theory. This included identifying risks, analyzing probability and impact, planning mitigation strategies, creating contingency plans, and monitoring risks throughout the project. The course emphasized that software projects often fail not only because of technical problems, but also because of poor planning, unclear scope, weak communication, unrealistic schedules, and unmanaged risks.

## Lab Work
The lab component focused on applying project management concepts to practical software project situations. The labs helped connect the theory from lectures to real project planning and team-based software development work.

Lab work included practicing how to break a project into smaller deliverables and tasks. This involved using Work Breakdown Structures to decompose a software project into manageable work packages. The purpose was to understand how project scope can be organized clearly before scheduling and assigning work.

The labs also involved creating activity lists and milestone lists. This helped show how project managers move from high-level project goals to specific tasks that can be scheduled, estimated, assigned, and tracked. Milestones were treated as important project checkpoints with no major duration or cost, but with strong importance for monitoring progress.

Another major lab area was schedule planning. This included sequencing activities, identifying dependencies, estimating task durations, and creating project schedules. Concepts such as finish-to-start, start-to-start, finish-to-finish, and start-to-finish relationships were used to understand how different tasks depend on each other.

The labs also involved working with schedule visualization tools such as Gantt charts and network diagrams. These were used to understand task order, project duration, task overlap, dependencies, deadlines, and schedule baselines. The goal was to learn how project managers track project progress and identify scheduling issues before they become larger problems.

Some lab activities focused on resource and cost planning. This included thinking about team member availability, task effort, project budget, resource allocation, and how delays or scope changes can affect the cost of a software project.

The course also included Agile-style lab work where students practiced team coordination, sprint-based progress, Scrum-style communication, and progress demonstrations. This helped reinforce how Agile teams organize work into smaller iterations, discuss blockers, review progress, and adjust plans based on feedback.

Overall, the lab work demonstrated how software project management is not only about documentation. It is also about making realistic plans, communicating clearly with team members, tracking progress, adjusting to change, and using structured methods to reduce uncertainty.

## Projects / Deliverables
The major project component involved working as part of a small software team to plan, manage, and deliver a software project using project management practices learned in the course.

The project required students to experience the full flow of a software project from planning to delivery. This included defining the project idea, identifying goals, organizing team responsibilities, planning the scope, estimating tasks, scheduling work, tracking progress, and presenting the final result.

One major deliverable was the project plan. This likely included the project scope, objectives, deliverables, stakeholders, schedule, milestones, risk considerations, and team responsibilities. The project plan acted as the central guide for how the team would execute and control the project.

Another deliverable was the Work Breakdown Structure. This broke the project into smaller deliverables and work packages so that the team could better understand what needed to be built and how the work should be divided.

The project also required schedule-related deliverables. These included activity lists, milestone lists, task dependencies, duration estimates, and Gantt chart-style schedules. These deliverables helped the team plan the work and track whether the project was progressing as expected.

The Agile/Scrum side of the project involved working in short development cycles. This included sprint planning, team check-ins, progress updates, and sprint demonstrations. The team had to communicate what was completed, what was planned next, and what obstacles were blocking progress.

The final project deliverable was a completed software application or software project presentation. This gave students the chance to demonstrate both the technical product and the project management process used to build it. The focus was not only on the final software, but also on how the project was planned, managed, coordinated, and delivered by the team.

## Key Takeaways
CPS714 showed that successful software projects require more than technical ability. A team can have strong developers, but the project can still fail if the scope is unclear, the timeline is unrealistic, risks are ignored, or communication is weak.

One major takeaway was the importance of planning before execution. A strong project plan gives the team a shared understanding of the goals, deliverables, timeline, responsibilities, and risks. Without this structure, software projects can quickly become disorganized.

Another key takeaway was that project scope must be managed carefully. Scope creep can affect the schedule, cost, quality, and team workload. This made it clear why requirements, scope statements, WBS documents, and change control are important in real software teams.

The course also showed that estimation is difficult but necessary. Duration estimates, effort estimates, three-point estimates, and PERT help teams reason about uncertainty instead of pretending every task will go exactly as planned.

Agile practices were also an important takeaway. Scrum meetings, sprint planning, sprint reviews, and retrospectives help teams stay aligned and adapt when requirements or priorities change. Agile is not just about moving fast, but about improving communication, feedback, and value delivery.

Another important lesson was that project managers need both technical understanding and people skills. They must understand the software being built, but they also need to manage expectations, communicate with stakeholders, handle risks, support the team, and make tradeoff decisions.

Overall, the course showed how project management connects technical development with real-world delivery. It helped build a stronger understanding of how software projects are planned, estimated, monitored, controlled, and completed in professional environments.
